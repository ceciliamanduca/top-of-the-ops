Show Notes

About This Episode

Ceci (FD at PortalOne, ex-VC at Talis Capital) and Bea (Chief of Staff at Praktika, ex-VC at Lakestar) open Season 2 with the episode nobody records. What gets talked about in ops is the shiny stuff: the systems you built, the processes you fixed, the tools you rolled out. This is the other half of the job. Firing people, restructuring, the board call that goes badly, the strategy everyone committed to that quietly isn't working, and the two people on the leadership team who cannot be in the same room.

Both hosts have run these processes from the operating seat and watched them from the board seat, and the episode uses both. Every story is drawn from past VC lives or things heard second-hand, not their current companies, as they say on the recording. It is the honest version, with the checklists attached.

What We Cover in This Episode

The First Person You Fire

Ceci's first was a one-on-one where the other person knew what was coming the moment the call started. Say it plainly ("this isn't working out and we have decided to let you go"), leave room for questions, bring HR in for notice periods and paperwork, and separate the person from the problem. Bea's confession is the more common one: she knew for six months and decided way too late, and she had not been upfront with continuous feedback, so it landed as more of a surprise than it should have. The rule they settle on: no more than a quarter between deciding in your head and acting, with a written line for yourself on what would change your mind.

Continuous Feedback Beats the Quarterly Review, but Do Both

Praktika runs quarterly reviews scored one to ten on values, deliverables, hard skills and communication. What made them work was the head of talent defining what a seven and a ten actually mean, because engineers cannot work off vibes. Bea once got a seven and had to ask the CEO what a ten looked like. Ceci's point is that a quarter is a long time in a startup: the structured review is where you think properly about someone's growth, and continuous feedback is what stops the exit conversation being a surprise. Everyone in the company should know what great means, from the interview onward.

The Lowest Common Denominator

Letting go late has been Bea's Achilles heel since moving into operations, and the cost is not the individual, it is the team. An overperforming team drifts to the lowest acceptable level; past a quarter, one person slacking pulls the whole average down. When she finally did it, a teammate's reaction was "it was coming, couldn't you do it before?" Ceci's test for the managers who come to her: has your gut ever been wrong? No. Then do it sooner. The only time it has surprised a team is when expectations were never clear in the first place.

The Exit Checklist

Start with the legislation of the country the person is employed in and what you can legally say, then work off a script prepared by legal or an external consultant. Decide in advance how teams get told in your company: leadership first and managers cascade, or a post in general. Then the mechanics: last day, handover expectations and deadline, immediate termination or garden leave, whether they get to say goodbye (if not, offer to send a message and their number on their behalf), notice, equity vested to the date, hardware buyout price, visa impact. And collect the laptop on time. One got lost in the ether for three months and the company kept paying the lease.

Tell the Team Immediately

Do not wait for the town hall. Send the Slack message the same day, with dignity and deliberately vague, because people already know and they lose trust in a company that is not transparent. Treat colleagues like adults and stop coddling them. Ceci's trick for the hours between a termination and the announcement: deactivate the email, then reactivate the Slack account so it looks alive but the person cannot log in. Useful, she notes, when you are doing several on the same day. The flip side both hold on to: someone who is not aligned with the company's values will be happier elsewhere, and keeping them is stealing their time.

Layoffs Start Before Anyone Hears About Them

The trigger is always a reckoning, or in Ceci's Italian, i nodi che vengono al pettine (the knots reaching the comb). Burn too high and sales too soft, a dysfunctional team, a board pushing hard, a first honest look at the runway, or a technology shift that makes a whole function redundant while the company is doing well. Then there is the one file: a big Excel of every cost, where names become rows and either get a termination date or do not. Bea has seen that file from the board seat, and her advice to every founder is to send the list back to your investors with references, contact details and what each person achieved, and ask them to place people across the portfolio. It is the biggest lever you have and the least you can do.

The Bare-Minimum Company

Ceci's method: err on cutting more rather than less, define the lights-on minimum, then build back up from it. Propose cost bases ("this is what 20 to 25 people costs, which 20 to 25 roles are we keeping?") rather than asking founders to strike names off a list. Keep the highly adaptable over the extreme specialist unless that specialism keeps the lights on. Bea's third option comes from a former boss in Silicon Valley: fire all 300 people including yourself, post 30 job specs, let everyone reapply, and pay severance to anyone who does not take a role. Roles first, then people. Ceci adds that a smaller company also needs a smaller operating cadence, or the meeting culture of the bigger company sinks the restructure.

Salary Cuts and the Poker Face

First to go are usually the overpaid executives and middle management. Before cutting a well-paid VP with a lot of equity, ask whether they would swap salary for equity, maybe at a 2x rate, as many founding teams did during COVID at 60% of salary. Should founders take a cut in a restructuring? Yes, unless they are already on very little; on a $250k salary while the company is going to shit, at least halve it. The hardest part is the weeks between the list and the day, when someone in a one-on-one asks "is my job safe?" Poker face, but drop enough breadcrumbs that a smart person starts looking, because someone resigning into a new job is better for everyone than being fired. Sometimes you have to lie to a friend. Both admit it changed their mood: Ceci gets tense and edgy executing a strategy she cannot soften for the people it hits; Bea copes by knowing she gave second chances and sent contacts to VCs.

Layoff Day and the Survivors

Run it as the tightest regime you have ever run: script and comms ready, a fast timeline, HR joining each call as it ends, IT blocking the account as HR joins, and the ops person making sure everyone is on Slack and on the calls at the right minute. Some jurisdictions require you to announce a proposed reduction in force before you know exactly who is affected. End the day with an all-hands and an honest CEO AMA. Then the part nobody plans for: the survivors. Treat them like adults, re-sell the strategy as if pitching the company from scratch, and get people together in person. Keep a list of the high-flight-risk people you cannot afford to lose and sell hardest to them. Restructures do work; one founder told Bea it was the best thing they ever did for the company.

When the Board Goes Wrong

Two failure modes from the investor seat, per Ceci: a board that has lost trust in the CEO, and a board member whose bad advice gets taken and shapes the company while you sit there with imposter syndrome thinking maybe they know better. Bea's first board set her standard for the worst: co-founders who could not agree a strategy, three investors with three incompatible theses (burn everything on marketing, spend only on what works, be EBITDA profitable now), one investor trying to remove the CEO, and a very young board observer playing telephone between people who would not speak. For founders: form a united front, and pick investors with similar principles.

Communicating Bad News to Investors

A good investor does their own due diligence and gets to the truth anyway, so be upfront: the bad news with the justification, over-communicated, in investor updates that keep getting longer. Ceci's model comes from Seqera's CEO Evan: take a day or two to breathe, then tell the lead investors directly with the why and the plan, so that by the board meeting it is old news and the meeting is about what to do next. A board can only help if it works from one truth. Early-stage investors have a ten-year window, so a blip in year two is irrelevant; pick the ones who know progress is not linear. Praktika runs no board deck, just a shared agenda document built collaboratively, and repeats the same five objectives to the board and the town hall until everyone is aligned. Repetita juvant (repetition helps).

Why an Ex-VC Chief of Staff Helps

Founders tend to give board members a halo and a crown. A chief of staff who has sat in the VC seat knows they are managers of capital with specific incentives, speaks their language, and can take an annoying board member's call and turn a conflict into "I understand why you're asking that". The market is full of ex-VCs who want to go to ops.

Pivots, Decision Gates and Feeling the Pull

Telling a strategy that isn't working from one that hasn't had enough time is as hard as executing it, and it is where early-stage VC training pays off: separating signal from noise on imperfect information. Ceci's tool is decision gates: not OKRs, just codified signals with dates. By Q1, have booked meetings for the new vertical gone up, is the messaging landing, has one person signed up. Bea's evidence is Praktika's B2B motion, which came purely from inbound support tickets they ignored for a while, then answered with a disgustingly ugly, pre-Claude MVP dashboard that nobody churned from. Praktika got to $20 million ARR with an ugly app; brand amplifies demand, it does not create it.

How Many Pivots Before People Stop Believing

Two at most. The first you can carry on vibes; the second needs real proof and the effort of an investment committee where the employees are the committee. Put as much work into communicating the strategy as into proving it, then get everyone to commit. Praktika runs disagree and commit: fight it out, leave aligned the same day, and keep your ego out of version one of the company. The operator's job is to concierge the process, and, Ceci adds, to be the bitch in the room asking the annoying-partner questions before the strategy goes out, so everyone knows where the holes are.

Conflict at the Top

Early signs: the founders stop being a united front, or one of them disengages. Bea watched two co-founders disagree openly at a board, which means the team is getting two executives saying two different things, a recipe for a company going to shit. When to step in? Bea's boundary is "not my circus": one attempt to reconcile them, and if they cannot disagree and commit the company will not win until someone leaves. Ceci looks for the cause first: a founder whose role feels threatened after new hires, one who disagrees on everything because they felt unheard on one thing, or founders who started as equals and are growing at different speeds. Restructure the org chart, give one founder clear ownership of what they are best at, let a co-founder become a superpowered IC. Anything unethical, and her job becomes removing that person. For conflict lower down, the best board advice Ceci ever got: lock the two in a room and don't let them out until it is hashed out. The chief of staff is the therapist who absorbs the anger first and only escalates what actually stops people working together.

High Performers Who Are Assholes

One of their previous bosses used to ask: why do you want to work with assholes? If the performance takes a toll on everyone else's, it is a net negative and people will leave because of that person. Bea let it happen once and it was a time bomb, the lowest common denominator applied to values. The feedback is blunt: if everyone has a problem with you, maybe the problem is you. Sometimes it is a written-communication problem, in which case run your Slack messages through AI before sending. Or, as Bea says, use Praktika.

How long should you give an underperformer before letting them go?

No more than a quarter once the decision is already made in your head, and write down for yourself what would change your mind. Extend to four or five months only if the person is finishing something critical that would take longer to redistribute, or if they tell you about a personal situation, in which case be more understanding, formally and informally. Both hosts say their mistake has been waiting too long, and that the rest of the team already knows.

What should be on the checklist when you let someone go?

Start with the employment legislation of the country the person is hired in and what you can legally say, then work off a script prepared by legal or an external consultant. Decide how the team gets told in your company. Then cover last day, handover expectations and deadline, immediate termination versus garden leave, whether they can say goodbye (if not, offer to send a message and their number on their behalf), notice, equity vested to the date, hardware buyout price, any visa impact, and collecting the laptop on time.

How do you tell the team someone has been let go?

Immediately, the same day, with dignity and deliberately vague. Do not wait for a town hall: people talk, and they lose trust in the company if it is not transparent. Treat colleagues like adults. If you need an hour or two between the conversation and the announcement, deactivate the person's email and reactivate their Slack account so it looks alive but they cannot log in.

What is a decision gate for a pivot?

A codified signal with a date attached, not an OKR or a goal. For example: by Q1, has the number of booked meetings for the new vertical gone up, is the messaging resonating, has one person signed up. Decision gates tell you whether to double down or ease off the gas, and they matter because pivots are decisions made on very little information.
