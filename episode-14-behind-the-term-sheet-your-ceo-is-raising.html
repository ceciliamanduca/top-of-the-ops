Show Notes

About This Episode

Ceci (FD at PortalOne, ex-VC at Talis Capital) and Bea (CRO at Praktika, ex-VC at Lakestar) spent years on the investor side sending diligence lists. Now they are the ones answering them, and it takes about ten times longer to answer one than to send it. This is the honest version of fundraising and M&A from the operator's seat, with no guest.

They go from the two ways a raise starts, preempted or going out, through the story, the deck and the data room, to time boxing the process, the sacrificial lamb, what the Chief of Staff actually does while the founder is out pitching, what changes after the term sheet, and how a bank-led M&A process runs. They finish on the mistakes that are easiest to avoid, on both sides of the table.

What We Cover in This Episode

Why Two Ex-VCs Can Talk About This

Ceci and Bea first worked together on an M&A process at a VC fund in the summer of 2020, Ceci on the Keynote, Bea on the Excel, long nights included. Between them they have done around 20 investments, sat at lead investor funds that were deep in portfolio management, and helped portfolio companies raise. Bea also spent three years doing M&A in a bank. What carried over into the operator seat, for Ceci: knowing how to build a story that resonates with VCs, which metrics to present and the story behind them, and the sequencing of who to approach first.

Keep Your Friends Close, but Your Buyers Closer

Banking taught Bea the opposite of what she now believes about M&A. In a bank-run process the company arrives with no idea who to sell to and the bank holds the relationships. In venture she learned, from her boss at Lakestar, that a good exit is a relationship built over years, because you never know when you will run out of cash and need someone to take the team. Ceci's view: early on, people buy you for the founder and the story, which is why M&A is the one thing a founder cannot delegate. And you owe it to your employees to find the business a good home, not just the highest bidder.

Preempted or Going Out, the Story Comes First

Praktika's Series A was preempted, so the team had a pitch deck, a product demo, a rudimentary model and a legal data room, and that was about it. When you go out to raise, the first and most important job is the story, and only the founder and CEO can set it. Investors are people who bond with a personal story, not three sentences about breaking the boundaries of a category. Practise the pitch like a TED talk, speak at 0.7x your normal pace, and never lean forward. Sit back and draw people in.

Bin the Frankenstein Deck

The common failure is taking the seed or Series A deck and patching new data into it for the B round. Put it in the bin and start from a blank page. Ceci writes the story with pen and paper first and builds the slides to fit the speech, not the other way round. The YC template is a starting point, not a rule. Find the accents, the numbers and anecdotes that make the story resonate, and back every claim with proof.

Who Writes It, and the Two-Deck Question

The founder writes the deck, because it has to sound like them. The Chief of Staff helps with the accents and the edit. Bea has not touched the Praktika deck in ages, and AI can help with design but not the words: AI slop irritates investors. On a short teaser deck versus the full one, Bea's take is that VCs differ, so send a lighter version first and keep the appendix for the live presentation. Her call to VCs: if you take the meeting, read the deck, check the website and Crunchbase, and if it is a consumer product, use it.

What Goes in the Data Room

It scales with stage. Before the term sheet there is the hygiene (accounts, cap table, legal documents) and the part investors dig into: a financial model with historicals plus three years forward, a KPIs file, cohorts for consumer, an anonymised pipeline for B2B, and a TAM analysis for an unfamiliar market. More funds now ask for raw transaction data to build their own cohorts, which is a lot to hand over pre-NDA. Post term sheet comes the full legal list: governing documents, board minutes, financing documents, IP, vendor contracts above roughly 20K a year, employment contracts, any disputes, and the stock plan. Ceci's addition: written companion documents, a deep dive on the go-to-market motion or the TAM, so raw numbers do not get misread. Storytelling again, through the numbers this time.

Time Box It

A raise takes the founder away from the business, so time box it. For pre-seed and seed Bea runs one month: warm-up calls, a week of back-to-back pitches, two weeks of diligence and Q&A, and a final week for term sheets. It creates competitive tension most companies do not have, and if nobody bites you extend. Ceci has set a deadline on a raise the company did not need, which doubled as a rule: if it is not done by then, come back when it is better.

The Sacrificial Lamb

Tier your funds and do not start with your favourite. Start with a term sheet you would reject anyway, string them along, and use that term sheet to catalyse everyone else, including your board. It is true, you have a term sheet. Should you ever fake one? No. Your board has to know, and once word gets out that you lied, nobody trusts you again. Would you take the lamb's money as a last resort? If it is a net neutral and the alternative is bankruptcy or a restructuring, yes, but keep them off the board. If you are only out because of momentum, do not.

Warm Intros, Partners and Associates

Ceci ran her fundraise CRM in Notion, Bea is fine with a Google Sheet, and neither would buy one. Warm intros are the currency, because the introducer vets the company, and cold outbound works like a sales funnel. Bea remembers a founder who refused to take a meeting with her as an associate and asked for the partner instead, and the answer was goodbye. Ceci's advice: research whether associates lead deals at that fund, get the junior to like you because they will run your diligence, then find out who takes it to IC. No network at all? Be loud on social media, go to founder circles and angel groups, or open Luma and search VC.

What the Chief of Staff Actually Does

For Ceci it was 80% of her job: coordinating every call, meeting and email, sitting in silently on pitches and slacking the CEO extra points live, then the first pass on diligence from a bank of questions, because VCs ask the same ones and you recycle 90% of the material. Bea's side was the financial one: cohorts, the model, the data room and the master Q&A document, closer to an acting CFO. Do you tell the company? Praktika and Seqera both did. In M&A you do not: with a public acquirer it is insider information, and employees cannot know what the buyer plans for them.

After the Term Sheet

Even before signing, the VC background helps: reading the terms, giving initial advice before the lawyers, and cap table modelling. After that it is project management the CEO should not be doing: coordinating lawyers, surfacing what matters, chasing replies and filling gaps in the data room. Both hosts have read shareholder agreements top to bottom, Bea first on a train from London to Edinburgh, and both say you have to. They are the rules of the game. Liquidation preference is first dibs.

Advisors, and How a Bank-Led M&A Works

An advisor replying instead of the founder in a fundraise is a massive ick. Bea would not love a Chief of Staff running the whole raise either. Ceci did the comms at Seqera early on, then handed the five funds that mattered back to the CEO: prep and draft, but the founder owns the relationship. M&A is different. The bank runs the teaser, the first calls and the NDA, then the model, a management presentation, first bids, targeted diligence, legal, second bids and a winner chosen on price or fit.

What Goes Wrong, and Final Tips

Bea: never let key numbers sit with one person. After 24 hours staring at a model you will not see the mistake, so get someone trusted to go cell by cell. Ceci: know your runway, burn and NRR inside out. To VCs, ask the three or four questions that matter rather than diligence for the sake of it, and growth investors should still ask about the product. Last tips: make sure the demo works (record it), rehearse the story arc, and on reply speed they disagree. Playing hard to get only works when you are being preempted. In a time-boxed raise, keep the momentum.
