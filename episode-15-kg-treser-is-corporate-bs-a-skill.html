Is Corporate BS a Skill?
A LinkedIn Chief of Staff Says Yes

What do you do when you can't put out all of the fires at once? Being a Chief of Staff at a large corporate is all about learning to have boundaries and knowing how to prioritise.

Show Notes

About This Episode

Kathryn (KG) Treser is Chief of Staff for Marketing at LinkedIn Talent Solutions. She started in strategy and operations consulting, spent seven years at LinkedIn moving up through business operations, and took the chief of staff seat in January 2024. She also writes Chief of Staff School on Substack.

Most guests on this show are the person at a startup who does the job because nobody else will. KG does it inside LinkedIn, inside Microsoft, where there are around 300 chiefs of staff. Ceci (FD at PortalOne, ex-VC at Talis Capital) and Bea (CRO at Praktika, ex-VC at Lakestar) ask her what the role looks like at that scale, and the answer keeps coming back to the same thing: you cannot put out every fire, so the job is deciding what reaches your boss, what you take on yourself, and what is okay to drop.

What We Cover in This Episode

A Chief of Staff at the Other End of the Scale

LinkedIn has several businesses inside it, and LinkedIn itself sits inside Microsoft. KG works in Talent Solutions, the part recruiters and talent leaders use to find talent, and reports to the person who is functionally that business's CMO, who in turn reports to LinkedIn's overall CMO. That makes her chief of staff to an L3, a very different job from chief of staff to the CEO. Getting anything done means product marketing, integrated marketing, global teams, centres of excellence, and cross-functional partners in finance and comms. A lot of her job is doing the first pass on an ask, getting the right people involved and giving a perspective.

Consulting, Then BizOps, Then the Seat

KG came out of a liberal arts degree without knowing Excel or how to build an executive presentation, and strategy and operations consulting taught her the basics. She wanted more depth with the people she worked with, so she moved into BizOps at a startup: partnerships, some HR, product launches. At LinkedIn, BizOps was much more specific, supporting the Talent Solutions sales team with a charter to help the business grow and run better. The skills that carried over: writing a crisp strategy, getting executive buy-in, and sourcing the data to back it.

Corporate Whack-a-Mole

As soon as you close one process, another pops up, and with AI and team changes it is getting worse. At LinkedIn there are hundreds of people working on how the jobs marketplace alone works, and the CEO does not need all of that. The chief of staff keeps leaders connected and informed, multiplied by a hundred. The skill is whacking the right moles first and closing things out properly, including telling someone who wants to get involved late that it is done, so it does not pop back up.

Deciding What Reaches Your Boss

"I don't want my boss to have the same volume of moles." KG's filter starts with being extremely clear on the three or so major initiatives her executive is across, the strategy, the things they do not want to be involved in and the things they have a bias towards, like a relationship with a particular executive. The rest is corporate acumen and trial and error. She once told her boss about rumours about their team on Blind. His answer: I do not care what is on that. Lesson learned.

Influence Without Authority

"I'm gonna bad cop this" is something they literally say. Sometimes the chief of staff title and a grounded perspective are enough for KG to lean in. Sometimes the move is drafting an email or setting up a meeting for her boss, because his name carries more weight than hers. And sometimes it is going to another chief of staff and suggesting their boss gets on something. She hates corporate politics and floundered in consulting partly because of it, but you still need to play politics for the organisation, and know when something cannot come from you.

Peer Chiefs of Staff, and Microsoft's 300

Every leader in LinkedIn's marketing organisation has a chief of staff, and they meet every two weeks with the CMO's chief of staff and her program office, which includes people leading AI transformation and annual planning. Mostly they share best practice, like the one-slider KG made when she felt the AI transformation kickoff was not enough, or how someone runs offsites. The honest tension is playing defence for her own team between centralised asks from the top and the marketing work that actually runs the business. Microsoft, she read, has 300 chiefs of staff. Most of the ones she knows are women, and she puts that down to EQ and an intuition for spotting problems. Bea adds low ego and a tolerance for invisible work.

Bill GPT

KG has a love-hate relationship with AI: highly dependent, and nervous about offloading things she wants to keep doing herself. Her role has little repetitive work, so AI is mostly a thought partner. Her ride-or-die is Bill GPT, built on transcripts of her boss's one-on-ones and meetings and the emails he has sent. Bill is a former founder who sold his product to LinkedIn and has a very distinct style, and anything she drafts on his behalf should sound like him. The day they recorded, she dumped a role and the backfill process into it and sent the email it wrote. Ceci did the same for her old CEO when the scientific writer they planned to hire turned the job down.

AI Transformation Inside an Enterprise

At a startup you hand out Claude seats and Granola. At LinkedIn there are strict compliance processes and security protocols, and getting a tool approved is a whole process. Granola no longer meets security requirements on corporate computers, and as part of Microsoft the teams are expected to use Microsoft AI products, so people who had been given Claude Code were switched to GitHub Copilot. The first phase was experimenting with the tools the marketing technology team picked. Now they are shifting to a few standard workflows everyone uses, and KG's job is shaping those workflows, finding who can help, unblocking, and making sure what marketing builds connects to sales.

The Teammate Who Won't Use AI

Bea asks about a team member who refuses to use AI for environmental reasons. KG is clear about where her job ends: she is not responsible for that person's career or review, and she understands the reservations. What she can do is help them find a small place to start, like using Copilot in Teams to summarise emails, while the organisation celebrates its super users. She expects AI usage to show up in performance reviews eventually. But if someone is doing a really good job and hitting their outputs, using AI for AI's sake would be silly too.

Boundaries, and Knowing What's Okay to Drop

Bea notices how clear KG's boundaries are. KG says she learned them over time, because taking accountability for everything led to exhaustion and to not caring, and she wants to care. At scale she cannot do everyone's work or shape their careers: "It's not white glove service. Do the process." The exception is her boss. If he wants something, she does it. Ceci describes the startup version, going from fixer to fixer-therapist: "I can't fix your mood this morning." On AI, Ceci caught herself reading summaries in four Claude chats and absorbing none of them, so anything priority one now gets read word by word. KG's 180 is similar: transcripts are great AI input, but only a few things from any meeting matter, and human discernment should find them.

When Your Principal Resigns

In a startup, if the principal goes, the chief of staff almost always goes too. KG's principal resigned for a compelling opportunity that could not include a chief of staff, and it was devastating: her whole working life had revolved around that person. She went through something like the stages of grief. Now she is glad it happened. The leader she supports now was promoted into the role, so she could bring over what had worked and help them ramp faster. It also reset her boundaries: "If I don't respond to a ping in ten minutes, the world will still turn."

Mind-Melding With a New Principal

Pay equal attention to what they say and what they do, because people describe themselves one way and put their energy somewhere else. Be human about the transition: what are you nervous about, here is what I am nervous about. Listen for the things they say again and again. Her new principal keeps saying he wants everyone to see themselves in the team priorities, so she now checks all-hands and team recognition through that lens. Ask for feedback after the first week, and expect it to take grit. Her real superpower: get their Enneagram report, and feed it into the GPT. KG is a two, the helper. Bea is a three, the achiever. Bea also mentions the how-to-work-with-me document from Claire Hughes Johnson's Scaling People, and Ceci wrote one when she got her first direct report.

Corporate Bullshitting, Defined

Executive presence plus improvisation. Sometimes you have to act more confident than you are, especially when you are leading a meeting or representing your team, because if you wait to feel perfectly prepared you will never be ready. You cannot know nothing, but you have to trust that you know things. Know your stories rather than reading your notes. Another chief of staff once told her it is a bit like being an actor. Ceci brings up the European version: the Silicon Valley startup with no revenue raising on confidence, and the profitable French company with 500 million in ARR saying it is doing okay. KG's coaching: force yourself to say something in a lower-stakes room that still makes you nervous, keep doing it, and remember we are on a floating rock in space.

Hire From Hospitality and Events

Every chief of staff job ad asks for banking or consulting. KG's view is that the trait that matters is grit, because the fires never stop, and events and hospitality build it with almost no margin for error. Her team runs what they call the Davos of Talent, and when a speaker overruns and the live stream is about to overlap, someone has to reorganise the whole conference and the meal schedule in two minutes. That is chief of staff work. Ceci agrees: waitressing and pub shifts were the most formative jobs she had. KG once got yelled at because an omelette was upside down.

Rapid Fire

Going viral on LinkedIn: KG does not know, but videos are getting more important, and you can now flag AI slop to get it out of your feed. What she got much better at: boundaries, taking care of the highest priority things and knowing what is okay to fall. The framework a 50-person startup should steal from a big corporate: a crisp vision, mission and values that everyone works from. Bea agrees, because founders assume everybody knows, and in reality nobody knows. Find KG at Chief of Staff School on Substack, on LinkedIn, and on TikTok as KG Chief of Staff.
