Inside a Frontier AI Lab
From First Check to Partnerships at Poolside

Andrea Badia Serra sent a cold LinkedIn message to a secretive AI lab, wrote the check, and then went and joined them. What business operations actually looks like at a frontier lab: three months of shadowing, a two gigawatt data centre nobody planned for, two investor audiences who want opposite things, and 20,000 experiments a month.

Show Notes

About This Episode

Andrea Badia Serra is Director of Partnerships and Business Operations at Poolside, the AI lab building frontier models for software engineering. She did investment banking at J.P. Morgan, then joined Lakestar, where Bea handpicked her and where she sat on the team that put money into Poolside. A few months after the investment she went and joined the company. Since January 2025 she has run capital markets, helped incubate a two gigawatt data centre company in West Texas, and, as of a few months ago, built the model partnerships function from scratch.

Most guests on this show own operations at one company. Andrea has spent the last year and a half operating at two. Ceci (FD at PortalOne, ex-VC at Talis Capital) and Bea (Chief of Staff at Praktika, ex-VC at Lakestar) take her through the whole arc: the diligence, the jump, the shadowing, the two jobs, and what it actually feels like to operate somewhere that cannot make revenue until it reaches the frontier.

What We Cover in This Episode

The Cold LinkedIn Message That Started It

Andrea was in VC in early 2022, when every AI lab was the hot category and she had looked at all of them. A friend tipped her off about another one raising $500 million at a $3 billion valuation. The company was secretive and there was nothing online, but one of the founders was Jason Warner, the former CTO of GitHub. She sent him a cold LinkedIn message and he replied within hours. That response is the first reason she invested: high talent density, kind-hearted and low ego. The second was how they worked. She expected a 10 page deck and received a 100 page FAQ answering every question she had.

Breaking Every Internal Rule to Write the Check

The signals an investor looks for were there: compute, go to market, a distribution plan, and a differentiated thesis focused on code rather than general purpose from day one. The icing was the investment committee. Lakestar broke its ownership threshold and its revenue threshold to write the check into a company that was pre-PMF, pre-revenue, pre-everything, and it got full votes in the room.

Turning Down the Obvious Job

A few months after the investment she had breakfast with Poolside's COO and explained she wanted to move to the operator side. She already had an offer in hand: one of the hottest growth stage startups in Europe, repeat founders, AI application layer, $50 million ARR, 3x year on year, tier one VC. Great on paper. The COO teased her: right role, right time, but why not join Poolside? Her first reaction was that she did not have the background and was not technical enough for an AI lab. The interview process is what changed her mind. The decision moment came at Web Summit in Lisbon, over breakfast with co-founder and now co-CEO Eiso, who asked in the last 30 seconds why she thought she was not the right person. She named the technical gap honestly, then pitched herself hard: fast learner, hard working, works under pressure, switches context fast, wants to get her hands dirty. The offer came a few days later.

The Checklist She Used to Pick a Startup

Becoming a founder was the goal from very young, and the plan was banking, then venture, then a startup. Her sister's advice after university was to get the experience and the stamp on the CV first, because the business will still be there. When the time came, the checklist was short: repeat founders, high talent density across every department rather than just leadership, a global mindset from day one, and a company solving a hard problem. Poolside's founders had written that humanity will climb three problems this century: space, energy and AGI. She joined to solve one and ended up on two. Last on the list was grit, a team with a winning mentality that does not mind being the underdog and does not quit when things get hard.

Three Months of Shadowing, Physically

On day one she did not know what BizOps meant or how it differed from chief of staff or founder's associate, and she did not care about the title. She cared who she was working for, which was the COO. The first three months were shadowing in the literal sense: one week a month in Madrid sitting next to her in her home, every other week in Paris with the team, and no conversation ruled too senior or too hard for her to sit in on. Partnerships calls, leadership strategy, compute, investor calls, org challenges, legal reviews. Her advice for anyone about to do the same: there is no homework that prepares you, so drop the pre-settled ideas and take every call at the same level of importance, including the ones that sound less glamorous than the big compute deal.

Never Ask the Same Question Twice

The one rule she carried over from banking. In the first months you can ask anything, so she forced herself to ask everything, sat down with people in Paris at the end of each day with the questions she had written down, and built a library of answers rather than asking again a month later. The balance she is still working: figure it out yourself, but put your hand up early when it is clearly something you do not know and it is not efficient to grind at it.

What She Had to Unlearn

From banking, the zero error standard. Nothing ships until it is perfect, every footnote read at 3am, every decimal checked. At Poolside, shipping at 90% today beats shipping flawlessly next week, because otherwise decisions get made without you. From VC, optionality: keeping every door open, multiple bets, no commitment. Here it is one bet with full conviction, going at it blindly, then adjusting fast when you are wrong.

Two Jobs, One Data Centre

Around March 2025, Eiso came up with what was then an insane idea: should we build our own data centre. Six people on the special project, so they divided and conquered. Andrea and one colleague, neither of whom had ever worked in infrastructure, had to put every land option next to every silicon option and work out which combination was viable. Their first call was to an investor from an infrastructure fund who had been through multiple data centre models and walked them through how one works. Two months, 90% of their time, one Excel. Once the decision was made, her colleague took the day to day of building it (land lease, long lead equipment, suppliers, silicon) and she took fundraising and investor relations for both businesses. Her involvement has come and gone in waves ever since. Today it is about three hours a week, until phase one financing closes and she can close the loop.

Two Audiences Who Want Opposite Things

The AI business sells to venture and growth capital: they underwrite the upside, tolerate ambiguity, and have usually seen it before, because they have backed other labs and understand that you have to underwrite a company's ability to reach the frontier before it gets there, which means pre-revenue. They know the ingredients are team, resilience, compute and the right bets. The infrastructure business sells to infrastructure private equity, and that is pure project financing: a signed lease, the quality of the tenant, downside protection in real assets like land and power, and every assumption stress tested to the decimal. At the beginning she felt she was good at neither. What it taught her is that you cannot pitch an infra investor with a vision slide, and you cannot pitch a growth investor with a DSCR table, so she learned to speak both languages fluently.

Organising Two Companies

Until January 2026 it was all one business in her head, one page of being vertically integrated from dirt to intelligence. Then the full spin-off into Poolside Infrastructure Company (PIC) forced a real separation: different Slacks, different CRM, different everything, an independent team, and for her two email addresses and two calendars. It was chaotic at first, and it got easier mostly because her involvement wound down when she picked up partnerships in April.

Volunteering Herself Into a New Function

On 28 April, after more than three years in the shadows, Poolside released its first models, and the job to be done changed. Before launch the constraint was capital and compute. After launch it was distribution and mind share. The models were live on OpenRouter, traffic was arriving from every direction, and nobody owned that surface or separated signal from noise. Engineers had got them listed on day one, but it was not their job. Andrea spotted the gap, pitched it to the COO, and asked for a trial month. People were confused, she had been investor lady and then data centre lady and now also partnerships, but she got the job officially.

Building the Partnerships Function From a Spreadsheet

Step one was a market map, the same exercise as in VC. She asked Poolside's own engineers which apps, agents and gateways they used day to day, because she was too far removed to know, then sorted the ecosystem into categories: inference providers, gateways, agent harnesses, benchmarks and evals providers, fine-tuning platforms. Step two was prioritisation, using ClickHouse, Poolside's internal database, to see where tokens were actually flowing, which surfaces mattered and where they were absent, then tiering the list one, two and three. Step three was instrumenting the motion: an Attio CRM for partners, replicated from the investor CRM she built in her first month, the whole org convinced to use it, a weekly partnerships meeting, a Slack channel with notes from every call, and an update in every all hands.

What a Frontier Lab Actually Feels Like

Poolside is the first startup she has ever joined, so she asks the veterans whether things are normal or just an AI lab thing. The flywheel is the strange part: you need model capabilities to make revenue, revenue to raise, the raise to buy compute, and compute to get the capabilities. Everything depends on the research team, and sales cannot sell without a good enough model. As the founders say, it all collapses into the model. Until they reach the frontier things happen in an unnatural order, and after that it becomes more of a normal company. Being comfortable with that was learned, not innate: her boss says they need to get the banker out of her, and she had to be reminded that she is not the founder and should be able to sleep at night.

Talent Density Without the West Coast

Researchers on the West Coast are being hired for more money than footballers, and Poolside decided not to compete there. The founders built a global team from day one instead, hiring on the East Coast, in Canada, across Asia and scattered around Europe, helped by having one founder from Canada and one from Europe who understood those markets. The result is one of the lowest churn rates in the industry, and most of the few who have left did not go to competing labs. The trade people are making is agency: a shinier salary and option package elsewhere, against actually being able to drive the experiments they want. Eiso's hiring bar is kind-hearted, low ego, ambitious and hard working. The one thing Andrea says they get wrong is being a little conflict-averse.

Remote, With Paris Three Days a Month

Fully remote, with three days together every month in Paris, mandatory. They fly people in from Brazil, Canada and across Europe, and rent the office out to other French startups the rest of the month. Paris is not because they are French, which Andrea says the press got wrong, they are American from day one. It is the logistics: most of Europe including the UK can reach it by train, and visas are far easier than the UK or the US for a global team. A London office opened about a year after she joined, and it is the only place with people in and out every day. More offices may come, but nobody will be forced in.

The Stack She Actually Uses

The VC rule she kept is never going into a call with a partner without having tried their product, which for partnerships means living in the terminal and running agents against Poolside's models and other models to understand what people mean when they say one harness has a different personality from another. On her bar: Hermes desktop app, OpenCode desktop app, the terminal with several agents running in parallel on the same question, Wispr Flow for dictation, Granola, Claude, ChatGPT, and Laguna, Poolside's own model. She checks each partner surface for a model picker and a recommended list she can get Poolside onto. The one thing she does not hand to AI is her Excels. She is one of only two people at Poolside of 180 without a Mac, builds models by hand on a Windows Surface, and pastes errors into Claude on the side rather than letting it near the file. Slides are fair game.

The Claude Skill Behind Her Queries

She has ClickHouse open every day with a dashboard of at least 20 graphs on token consumption by source and by paid versus free endpoint, and it drives most of her partner conversations. Rather than keep writing and fixing SQL, she built a Claude skill that breaks down every customer ID in the data lake and classifies them into the four categories she cares about, so the queries come back consistent and without errors.

Model Factory

Most of the compute a big AI lab spends goes on experimentation, not the headline training run. Andrea cites an Epoch AI piece: of roughly $7 billion OpenAI spent on compute in 2024, $2 billion went to inference and $5 billion to R&D, and of that $5 billion only about $400 million went to the large training run for GPT-4.5. The rest was experimentation. The large run is fixed by physics and roughly the same for every lab; the difference is how many experiments you can run, because model building is trial and error. With a smaller team and less compute than competitors, Poolside industrialised it early into what they call the model factory, an automated assembly line where every experiment is tracked and reused for the next run. Her other claim: model building is 90% an engineering problem, not a research problem, and their engineers sit next to the researchers making it faster. The output is 10,000 to 20,000 experiments a month, a model trained from scratch in eight to nine weeks on 4,000 H200s with a team under 100 people, and a previous model trained in five. Bea's read: constraints are what make people innovate.

Rapid Fire

Most useful thing from banking: not a skill, a mentality, hard work and the ability to work under pressure. What VCs get wrong about operating: they think joining a startup is about strategy. Strategy comes from the founders and leadership, the rest is execution, and you grind in the same direction. Is business operations as a title here to stay: yes, because people want more specificity than generalist chief of staff or founder's associate. Advice for VCs who want to jump: diligence the company like it was an investment of your own money, because it is one, your time. The team matters more than the idea or the market. And do not wait for the perfect moment. When your shoe cleaner tells you to buy a stock it is too late, and if everyone is already talking about a company, you have probably missed the upside.

Model Factory

Bea [36:58]

One last question before wrapping everything up. Can you tell us a little bit more about model factory, what it is at Poolside and how are you involved?

Andrea [37:08]

Many people are not aware of this, but most of the compute spent for big AI labs goes towards experimentation. So there is this article I really like from Epoch AI that says that OpenAI spent 7 billion in compute in 2024. 2 billion of that went to inference, so people consuming tokens from their models. The other five went to R&D. Out of those five, only 400 million went to the large training run for ChatGPT 4.5. The remaining, up to 5 billion, was experimentation.

What does this mean? The large training run is fixed by physics. There is only so much compute you need for a specific size of a model, and it's going to be the same for every lab. The big difference is experimentation. And this is because model building is essentially a science of trial and error. And the more compute you have, the more experiments you can run, the faster you can iterate and the quicker you can get to the right recipe, as we call it.

So given that we are a much smaller team than some of our competitors and have much less compute than some of our competitors, we had to find smart ways on how to be very efficient with experiments. And the way experiments are done at other labs is artisanal. So they run ad hoc experiments one at a time. They're bottlenecked by people. So the more people, more experiments. And so what we have done is very early on, we industrialised the way that we do model building and we call it the model factory. So this is literally an automated assembly line for model building. So every experiment is tracked and reused and can be used for the next model run.

Another big secret is that model building is 90% an engineering problem and not a research problem. And at Poolside, apart from having great researchers, we have great engineers. And so they have been sitting side by side with the researchers, looking at how they build models and helping them do it faster. So the result today is that we are running 10,000 to 20,000 experiments a month. We trained our model from scratch in eight to nine weeks with only 4,000 H200s, with a team of less than 100 people. And our previous model was trained in only five weeks. So the asset right now for us is that speed and efficiency on how we train models.
